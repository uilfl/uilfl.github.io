## Beyond optimization

There is a line from Robert Frost:

> I shall be telling this with a sigh
> Somewhere ages and ages hence:
> Two roads diverged in a wood, and I—
> I took the one less traveled by,
> And that has made all the difference.

I keep coming back to it because of what I see in my own work. I do industrial design in e-commerce, and the honest read is that the field is stuck in optimization — squeezing conversion, shaving friction, iterating on the same funnel. That's not nothing, but it isn't growth. It's the road most traveled, and I don't think it's where the next real work is.

I believe research on technology changes how society works, how business runs, and how people understand the world — and that belief is what's pulling me off the optimization path. As AI becomes accessible to everyone, I think the discipline best positioned to make sense of that shift isn't pure engineering. It's HCI: the field that already sits at the intersection of social science, engineering, and natural science, built to ask what a technology does to the people using it, not just whether it works.

That's the unconventional path, for me: choosing to study the interface instead of only optimizing it.

## When agents do the work, alignment is the work

Here's where that belief stopped being abstract. Uber Engineering [recently described](https://x.com/UberEng/status/2093444169037762840) their internal state: more than 70% of pull requests now attributed to local or cloud agents, over 3,600 agent skills built across the software development lifecycle, and 30,000+ agent skill executions a day. A growing share of sessions aren't started by a human at all — they're managed agents handling code review, self-healing CI failures, completing end-to-end PRs with visual validation, triaging on-call alerts, debugging incoming bugs, with humans stepping in for review and escalation.

That last part is the part I care about. When agents handle execution, the human's job shrinks to the review and escalation boundary — deciding what to trust, what to check, what to hand back. That boundary is a human-computer interaction problem, not a model-capability problem. From my own experience building with and around agents, I've felt how easy it is for that boundary to be badly designed — for "human in the loop" to mean a rubber stamp instead of real judgment. Getting that boundary right, the alignment between what agents do and what humans actually need to verify, is a design problem before it's anything else.

AI is also lowering the barrier to doing research at all — more people can investigate hard problems than could five years ago. I think that raises, not lowers, the bar for what it takes to reach mid-senior roles in tech: the differentiator stops being "can you build it" and starts being "do you understand the people and the agents well enough to know when the output is right."

I don't know yet whether that's a net good. Gresham's Law says bad money drives out good — cheap, fast, agent-generated work could crowd out slower, more careful, well-aligned work if the review layer doesn't hold. I've been in this field less than five years, so I'll say plainly that I don't have the answer to that. But it's the open question I want to spend my research on.
